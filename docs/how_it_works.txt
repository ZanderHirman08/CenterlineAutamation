HOW THE CENTERLINE ALIGNMENT TOOL WORKS
=======================================

THE BIG PICTURE
---------------
The tool does not trace roads from scratch. It starts from your existing road
lines and nudges each one to the middle of the pavement it can see in the
imagery. There are two main parts:

  1. Learn what pavement looks like in your imagery (training)
  2. For each road, find the pavement edges and center the line between them
     (alignment)


PART 1: LEARNING WHAT PAVEMENT LOOKS LIKE
-----------------------------------------
Training labels come from your own road file. The tool assumes your local
roads are roughly right (within a few feet), so:

  - Pixels within 4 ft of a road line are labeled "road."
  - Pixels 45-120 ft from any road are labeled "not road."
  - Everything in between is left out, because it is unclear (shoulders,
    driveways, yards).

It samples these from a few 512 x 512 ft areas in a handful of grids. That is
the "Training on grid(s): 137, 244" line in the run messages.

For each pixel it looks at about 13 measurements, not just color:

  - The red, green, blue and near-infrared values
  - Brightness, and how gray the pixel is (pavement is gray; grass and trees
    are colored)
  - A vegetation index from the near-infrared band (plants reflect
    near-infrared strongly; pavement does not)
  - Blurred versions of these, plus local texture, so each pixel also "sees"
    its surroundings

It then fits a simple statistical model (logistic regression). The model
learns how much each measurement counts toward "road." In the Glynn test run,
road pixels averaged brighter and lower in near-infrared (0.53) than non-road
pixels (0.62). The 0.81 accuracy means it labeled about 81% of the training
pixels correctly.

The model is saved as road_classifier.json in the work folder, so it is only
trained once per run.


PART 2: ALIGNING EACH ROAD
--------------------------
A road-probability map. For each grid, the tool applies the model to the
imagery near the roads. Every pixel gets a 0-1 score for "how much this looks
like pavement." That is what the optional road_prob_<grid>.tif shows: bright
where it thinks there is road.

Cross-sections. For each road line, every 10 ft it draws a short line across
the road, 40 ft to each side, and reads the probability along it, like taking
a slice through the road:

    probability   ___-#########-___
                  <-- 40 ft --|-- 40 ft -->
                     left edge  right edge
                         \-- midpoint = measured center

Fitting a ribbon (the default method). Instead of judging each
cross-section on its own, the tool treats the road as a ribbon: at every
cross-section it has a center and a width. It tries every combination of
center (within 30 ft, in 1 ft steps) and width (10 to 60 ft) at every
cross-section, and picks the one path through all of them that best:

  - covers pixels that look like road and leaves out the rest,
  - bends gently (the center can't jump sideways between cross-sections),
  - keeps a steady width, near the road's typical width.

Narrowing below the typical width costs more than widening, because a tree
shadow or overhanging crown makes pavement look narrower, never wider. So
when a shadow hides one edge for a while, the ribbon keeps its width and
stays centered instead of sliding toward the sunny side. A grey parking lot
or driveway next to the road would need a sudden widening, so it barely
pulls the line either.

Cross-sections within 30 ft of an intersection are ignored, because they
look along the cross street. The ribbon carries through them from the
cross-sections on either side, which removes the bends at intersections.

Where nothing usable was found (canopy, no imagery), the line stays where it
was and gets flagged. It never guesses.

The older method is still there (Advanced > Refinement method > midpoint):
it finds the two 50% edges at each cross-section, takes their midpoint, then
drops outliers and smooths.

Keeping the network connected. Each road is processed on its own, so where
roads meet their ends could drift apart. The tool works out where the
corrected roads actually cross and moves all the ends to that point. It
blends that move back over 50 ft so the rest of each line is not bent. The
same thing happens where a road is cut at a grid edge.


OPTIONAL EXTRAS
---------------
LiDAR. make_lidar_layers.py turns the USGS LAZ tiles into three rasters per
grid: the laser return strength of ground points (asphalt reads differently
from grass), the ground elevation (road beds are flat and smooth) and the
surface elevation (minus the ground gives tree height). With those, the
classifier can find pavement where the imagery only shows tree tops, and it
learns to trust the imagery less where trees are tall.

Second pass. After a full run, a new run can learn from the first one's
confident lines: it labels their full measured width as road and the verges
just outside them as not-road, which is far better training data than "within
4 ft of the original line".

Measuring. "Evaluate Against Checked Roads" compares any road layers with the
roads you validated by hand, every 5 ft, and reports the error by cover type
(open, shadow, partial trees, canopy, intersection).


THE OUTPUT FIELDS
-----------------
  Field                 Meaning                                   Glynn test
                                                                  (median)
  SHIFT_AVG/SHIFT_MAX   How far the line moved, on average and    3.3 / 7.6 ft
                        at most (ft)
  PCT_VALID             Share of cross-sections that found        62%
                        pavement
  WIDTH_MED             Typical pavement width measured           24 ft
  CONF                  Pavement contrast x share found.          0.26
                        Higher means a clearer road
  REVIEW / REASON       1 = check this line by eye, and why

Lines are flagged for review if any of these apply:

  - low_confidence: CONF below 0.2
  - few_matches:    pavement found on fewer than 30% of cross-sections
  - large_shift:    moved more than 15 ft somewhere
  - no_imagery:     missing imagery


WHERE ACCURACY IS LOST
----------------------
  - The training labels are a little wrong. The local roads are a few feet
    off, so some "road" training pixels are really road shoulder. That makes
    the model less sure where the edges are.
  - Measuring one pixel at a time is limited. Concrete can look like a
    rooftop, and shadows look like asphalt.
  - Trees hide the pavement. No method can measure what the imagery does
    not show.

Possible improvements that target those points:

  - Second pass: retrain on the corrected lines, for cleaner labels.
  - Hand-drawn pavement samples: perfect labels in the hard spots.
  - Pavement outlines (edge-of-pavement or impervious-surface data): skip
    the guessing entirely where they exist.
  - Accuracy check against digitized points: know whether a change actually
    helped.
